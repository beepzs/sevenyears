Skin & eczema

Is my child’s eczema really a gut problem?

Joy McCarthyGuest · Holistic nutritionist
Published 18 Sep 2026 Reviewed 25 Sep 2026 Pending clinical review

The short answer

Partly — and it is worth knowing why. Eczema happens when the skin barrier is leaky and the immune system over-reacts. Research increasingly links both to the gut microbiome, especially in the first years of life. Good skin care still comes first; supporting the gut can sit alongside it.

What eczema actually is

Eczema (atopic dermatitis) is a combination of two things: skin that does not hold moisture well, and an immune system that reacts strongly to everyday triggers. The result is the dry, itchy, inflamed patches most parents recognise — often in the creases of elbows and knees, or on the cheeks in babies.

Where the gut comes in

Most of the immune system is trained in the gut during the first years of life. Studies have found differences in the gut bacteria of babies who go on to develop eczema, and early-life factors that shape the microbiome — such as birth by C-section or early antibiotics — are associated with a higher chance of eczema. This is what people mean by the gut–skin axis.

That does not mean eczema is only a gut problem, or that changing diet alone will clear it. It means the gut is one lever among several.

What parents can reasonably do

  • Protect the skin barrier every day. Short lukewarm baths, then a thick fragrance-free moisturiser within a few minutes.
  • Notice triggers. Heat, sweat, scratchy fabrics, soaps and sometimes specific foods can all flare skin.
  • Feed the microbiome. A varied diet with fibre from fruit, vegetables, oats and beans, plus fermented foods such as plain yogurt if your child tolerates them.
  • Ask before you add. Probiotics have mixed evidence for eczema — talk to your pediatrician about whether a specific product makes sense for your child.

When to see your doctor

Book an appointment if the skin is weeping, crusting or very red (it may be infected), if your child has a fever alongside a flare, if eczema is disturbing sleep, or if it is not improving with good daily care. Never stop a prescribed treatment without speaking to your doctor first.
